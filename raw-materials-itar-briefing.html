For Beginners — No Jargon

Where these metals actually come from, and what "milling" means

You don't need an engineering degree for this — just the big picture. Every metal below goes through the same basic journey: it's dug out of the ground somewhere, melted and cleaned up, then squeezed, rolled, or pulled into a usable shape. Here's that journey in plain English, then the specifics for each metal SpaceX cares about.

"Milling" just means shaping metal. A "mill" is a factory that takes a raw block of metal and rolls, presses, or pulls it into the flat sheets, round bars, or wires that engineers actually build with. Nobody ships a raw lump of titanium — it always goes through a mill first.
1. Dig it up

Mining companies pull raw ore out of the ground — this is where "who mines it" comes in.

2. Melt & purify

The ore is melted and cleaned up until it's a pure block of metal, called an "ingot" (or "sponge" for titanium).

3. Shape it (milling)

A mill rolls it flat (plate/sheet), pushes it through a die (extrusion), or pulls it thin (wire).

4. Ship it

The finished plate, bar, sheet, or wire is sold to companies like SpaceX to build parts from.

Ti

Titanium

The one to know cold — very few places on Earth can actually make it
Dug up inOre is mined in several countries, but that's not the bottleneck — turning it into usable metal is.
Melted & purified byMostly Japan (Osaka Titanium, Toho Titanium) and Kazakhstan (UKTMP). China makes the most volume (~70% of world supply) but can't sell into Western aerospace — no certification. Russia (VSMPO-AVISMA) used to be a top aerospace supplier but Western buyers have been dropping them since 2022.
This step is calledMaking "sponge" — titanium doesn't melt into a normal liquid puddle, it comes out looking like a spongy, porous block. That sponge then gets re-melted into a solid ingot before it can be rolled into bar or plate.
Why it matters here: there are only a handful of mills on Earth qualified to supply aerospace titanium, so SpaceX cares enormously about who you know in that short list.
Ni

Nickel

One country produces more than everyone else combined
Dug up inIndonesia — by a wide margin (about 2.2 million tons/year). The Philippines, Russia, Canada, and Australia are distant runners-up.
Two ore types"Laterite" ore (soft, found in tropical places like Indonesia) and "sulfide" ore (hard rock, found in Canada and Russia) — they need different processing methods to get the metal out.
Turned intoRefined nickel or "ferronickel," then alloyed with other metals to make high-temperature alloys like Inconel and Monel, which get rolled or extruded like any other metal.
Why it matters here: Indonesia's export policy alone can swing world nickel supply — it's a single-country risk story, similar to titanium's mill bottleneck.
Al

Aluminum

Not about mining — it's about who has cheap electricity
Starts asBauxite ore, refined into a white powder called alumina, then "smelted" (melted with a huge electric current) into metal.
Dominant producerChina makes about 43 million tons/year — more than every other country combined. India, Russia, Canada, and Gulf states like the UAE follow, mostly because smelting takes enormous amounts of electricity and those places have cheap power.
Easiest to shapeAluminum is the friendliest metal to extrude (push through a die), which is why it's the default choice for complex structural parts.
Why it matters here: aluminum supply risk is mostly about electricity prices and tariffs, not mine scarcity — a very different risk conversation than titanium.
Cu

Copper

A two-country story: Chile and Peru
Dug up inChile (about 27% of the world's copper, ~5.3 million tons/year), then the Democratic Republic of Congo and Peru. China, the US, and Indonesia round out the top producers.
Purifying stepMined ore is smelted and then "electrolytically refined" — basically an electric bath that pulls out impurities — into pure copper cathodes (flat purified sheets used as the starting material).
Shaped byRolling into sheet, or "drawing" (pulling through smaller and smaller holes) into wire — copper's most common form.
Why it matters here: DRC's Kamoa-Kakula mine is a real, current watch item — a disruption there ripples through global copper supply fast.
SS

Stainless Steel

Not mined at all — it's a recipe, not a raw material
Made fromIron ore + chromium (for rust resistance) + nickel (for the common "300-series" grades like 304 and 316), melted together — not dug up as "stainless steel" anywhere.
Melted inAn electric arc furnace — think of a giant version of an electric stove burner, but hot enough to liquefy steel.
Shaped & finishedHot rolled, then cold rolled for a precise thickness, then "annealed" (carefully reheated and cooled) and acid-cleaned to build the thin invisible layer that actually makes it rust-proof.
Why it matters here: since it's a recipe, the real question isn't "where was it mined" — it's which mill melted and certified this specific batch to aerospace spec.
Cheat Sheet

The 5 shaping words you'll hear in the interview

Rolling (hot & cold)
Squeezing metal between big rollers, like a pasta maker, to make it flat — hot rolling for thick plate, cold rolling for thin, precise sheet.
Extrusion
Pushing heated metal through a shaped hole (a "die"), like a Play-Doh extruder — makes bar, tube, and complex shapes.
Drawing
Pulling metal through smaller and smaller holes to make wire — the metal gets thinner and longer each pass.
Forging
Hammering or pressing a hot metal block into shape (no rollers) — used for extra-strong parts like landing gear.
Ingot / Sponge
The raw, solid starting block of pure metal before it's shaped. "Sponge" is titanium's version — it looks porous, not liquid-smooth.
Mill
The factory that does the rolling/extruding/drawing. When someone says "mill relationships," they mean who you know at these factories.

Part 2

ITAR — the basics I need to speak fluently

The International Traffic in Arms Regulations, administered by the State Department's Directorate of Defense Trade Controls (DDTC).

04 What ITAR actually controls

ITAR governs the export of defense articles and defense services, and the technical data associated with them, as defined on the U.S. Munitions List (USML). It's administered by the State Department (DDTC) — separate from EAR (Export Administration Regulations), which covers dual-use/commercial tech through the Commerce Department and the Commerce Control List.

ITAR → State Dept / DDTC EAR → Commerce Dept USML vs. CCL

Two USML categories matter directly for a SpaceX-style role: Category IV (Launch Vehicles, Guided Missiles, Rockets — including rocket motor cases and specially designed parts) and Category XV (Spacecraft and Related Articles, updated in Sept. 2025 to reflect commercial space growth, though satellites/components meeting its performance criteria remain controlled). Raw metal stock itself usually isn't controlled by grade alone — it becomes controlled once it's processed into a "specially designed" part for a controlled end-item, which is exactly why the sourcing manager's job is to know where that line sits.

05 "Technical data" and deemed exports

ITAR's reach extends past physical goods to technical data — drawings, specs, and manufacturing know-how. A deemed export happens when that data is shared with a foreign national inside the U.S. — it's treated as if it were physically exported to that person's home country, even though nothing crosses a border.

Practical triggers I'd need to watch for: sending technical drawings to a foreign supplier for manufacturing review, letting a foreign-national coworker view controlled engineering drawings on-screen, hosting a foreign supplier rep on the production floor, or discussing controlled specs with an H-1B colleague without proper authorization. None of these require a border crossing to count.
06 Penalties — why this isn't optional

Per-instance exposure: civil fines up to $500,000, criminal fines up to $1,000,000, and up to 10 years imprisonment. The government can also bar a company from future import/export activity entirely — an existential risk for a defense/aerospace supplier, not just a fine.

Part 3 — the part most sourcing candidates miss

The DFARS Specialty Metals Clause (252.225-7009)

This is the regulation that actually touches raw material purchasing decisions day to day — more directly than ITAR does.

DFARS 252.225-7009 restricts DoD contractors from using specialty metals in certain articles unless the metal was melted or produced in the United States or a "qualifying country." This is often discussed alongside the broader Berry Amendment, which requires domestic sourcing of specific materials (including specialty metals, textiles, and food) for DoD purchases.

Metals covered

Steel & stainless steel Titanium alloys Nickel / nickel-iron alloys (Inconel, Monel) Aluminum alloys

What compliance requires

Full batch traceability, mill certifications, production records, and test reports proving domestic (or qualifying-country) melt — retained and audit-ready throughout the product lifecycle.

Why this is my strongest bridge into the raw-metals gap: "domestic melt traceability" is functionally the same discipline as the cGMP batch-traceability and mill-certification work I already do qualifying raw-ingredient suppliers at Rodan & Fields — same rigor, different form of paperwork.

Part 4

The five compliance blind spots procurement teams actually get burned on

1. Supplier oversight gaps

Assuming Tier 2/3 suppliers self-screen. Regulators hold the whole chain accountable, not just the prime.

2. Fragmented global process

Different sites using inconsistent screening tools creates uneven compliance execution.

3. Incomplete screening scope

Freight forwarders and routing intermediaries need vetting too, not just the named supplier.

4. Jurisdictional misreads

Non-U.S. companies are not automatically exempt — re-export of U.S.-origin content still triggers U.S. control.

5. Documentation gaps

"No documentation means no compliance," regardless of intent — screening has to be provable, not just done.

Beyond The Basics

Technical glossary & the 4-point material evaluation checklist

The vocabulary and QA framework that comes up once the conversation goes past "where's it from" into "how do I know this specific batch is good."

What is "Finish"?

All secondary thermal, chemical, and mechanical operations applied to raw metal stock after primary forging or hot rolling: heat treatment/annealing (baking in a controlled atmosphere to relieve internal stress and hit target strength), surface conditioning (grinding, scalping, or acid pickling to remove oxide scale and micro-cracks), and dimensional sizing (machining or cold-drawing to tight tolerances).

What is "NDT"?

Non-Destructive Testing evaluates internal and surface integrity without damaging the stock: ultrasonic/immersion testing (sound waves check bar/plate volumes for internal voids or inclusions), liquid penetrant & eddy current (detect surface cracks), all recorded on a certified Material Test Report (MTR) for traceability.

4-Point Material Evaluation Checklist

1. Material certification & chemistry (MTR)
Verify chemical composition and mechanical properties (tensile, yield, elongation) against engineering spec.
2. Traceability & chain of custody
Track every bar/sheet back to the specific melt heat number — complete provenance for failure analysis.
3. Geometry & yield loss pattern
Compare standard mill stock dimensions against cut-nesting drawings to minimize discarded "drop" scrap.
4. Mill utilization & uptime audit
Evaluate furnace uptime, lead times, and capacity utilization so the mill doesn't bottleneck production ramps.

Advanced Glossary

VAR (Vacuum Arc Remelting)
Secondary melt pass under vacuum to remove gas and inclusions — common for titanium and steel.
VIM (Vacuum Induction Melting)
Primary melt technique for nickel superalloys like Inconel.
BTF (Buy-to-Fly Ratio)
Weight of raw material purchased vs. weight of the finished flight part — a high ratio means a lot of expensive scrap.
Revert / Scrap Reclaim
Segregated clean machining scrap returned to the mill for a purchase credit.
LTA (Long-Term Agreement)
Multi-year contract securing mill capacity allocation and pricing ahead of a production ramp.
Part 5

Quick reference — if I only remember five things

ITAR vs. EAR

ITAR = military/defense articles, State Dept, USML. EAR = dual-use/commercial, Commerce Dept, CCL. Both apply globally when U.S.-origin content is involved.

Deemed export

Sharing controlled technical data with a foreign national on U.S. soil counts as an export to their home country — no border crossing required.

Titanium risk

VSMPO-AVISMA (Russia) still supplies ~35% of Boeing's and 50%+ of Airbus's titanium. ATI and TIMET are the U.S. alternatives engine makers are shifting toward.

DFARS specialty metals

DoD articles generally require steel/titanium/nickel/aluminum melted in the U.S. or a qualifying country, with full batch traceability — my cGMP audit muscle transfers directly here.

Penalties

Up to $500K civil / $1M criminal fines and 10 years per instance, plus potential export privilege revocation for the company.

My honest gap

No direct ITAR experience — but deep regulated-industry discipline (FDA/cGMP/ISO 13485) that ramps fast, and I say so plainly rather than overclaiming.
